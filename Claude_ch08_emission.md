## Section 14: Elastic Rebound, the Dynamic Event Boundary, and the Unified Emission Spectrum

### 14.1 Motivation and Scope

Section 13 established that the originating node has a real physical surface with finite radius $R_0$, bulk stiffness $K$, and a universal critical mass ratio $\mathcal{R}_c$ governing the eruption trigger. This section extends that framework to the dynamic behavior of the node boundary under accumulating strain, derives the rebound mechanism as the classical replacement for Hawking radiation, and shows that the same elastic process — operating across different strain regimes — unifies black hole emission, active galactic nuclei, quasar variability, and the eruption event itself into a single physical mechanism.

The results are:

1. Black holes do not evaporate. The thermal emission attributed to Hawking radiation is the statistical result of elastic rebound at a physical node surface — a classical mechanism requiring no quantum field theory, no virtual particle pairs, and no event horizon pair production.

2. The event boundary is not a fixed geometric surface defined by escape velocity equaling $c$. It is a **dynamic threshold** that shifts outward as node strain increases toward $\mathcal{R}_c$.

3. The node telegraphs the approaching eruption through a measurable crescendo of increasing emission — hotter, more energetic, higher rate — as strain accumulates. This crescendo is observable.

4. The full zoo of energetic astrophysical emitters — active galactic nuclei, quasars, blazars, Seyfert galaxies — are nodes at various stages of strain accumulation, not geometrically special accretion configurations. Their spectral diversity reflects strain state diversity, not structural diversity.

### 14.2 The Rebound Mechanism: Classical Elastic Scattering at a Physical Surface

In standard General Relativity the black hole has no physical surface. The event horizon is a causal boundary defined by the condition that escape velocity equals $c$, and the singularity at the center has zero volume and infinite density. Hawking radiation is derived by quantizing fields in the curved spacetime near this causal boundary, producing virtual particle pairs at the horizon with one partner falling inward and one escaping outward. The mechanism is quantum mechanical and requires no physical surface.

The Claude Theory requires a physical surface. The vacancy field $\phi$, with its finite wall thickness $\delta_w$ and finite spatial scales set by $(\epsilon_0, \lambda, v)$, cannot have a structureless point as its source. The node interior — where $\phi \to 0$ — has a real boundary at $R_0$ with real elastic properties. This changes the emission mechanism fundamentally.

When infalling mass strikes the node surface at $R_0$ it encounters a restoring pressure:

$$P_{elastic}(\xi) = K \cdot \xi = \frac{8\lambda v^2}{R_0} \cdot \xi$$

The collision is elastic. The infalling mass rebounds. If the rebound velocity $v_r$ exceeds the local escape velocity:

$$v_{esc}(R_0) = \sqrt{\frac{2GM_{point}}{R_0}}$$

the particle escapes to infinity. If not, it falls back and contributes to the next impact cycle.

The thermal spectrum of escaping particles follows directly from the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution of infall velocities in the node's gravitational potential well. No quantum mechanics required. The effective temperature of the emission:

$$T_{eff} = \frac{G M_{point} m}{k_B R_0}$$

where $m$ is the characteristic mass of the rebounding particle and $k_B$ is Boltzmann's constant. This is structurally identical to the Hawking temperature $T_H = \hbar c^3 / 8\pi G M k_B$ — both predict thermal emission inversely proportional to node mass — but derived entirely from classical elastic scattering statistics rather than quantum field theory in curved spacetime.

**The distinction matters physically.** Hawking's derivation predicts the black hole loses mass continuously and evaporates completely in finite time. The elastic rebound mechanism predicts no such thing. The node does not evaporate. It accumulates. The escaping fraction is real mass lost from the node's gravitational catchment — the $\epsilon'$ term from Chapter 7 — but the node itself persists, compresses, and trends toward $\mathcal{R}_c$. Emission and accumulation proceed simultaneously. The node is not dying. It is loading.

### 14.3 The Dynamic Event Boundary

In standard GR the event horizon is fixed at the Schwarzschild radius:

$$R_s = \frac{2GM}{c^2}$$

It is a geometric surface, determined entirely by mass, that does not move as the black hole accretes or emits.

In the Claude Theory the event boundary is the surface at which rebound energy equals escape energy. Define the **rebound escape radius** $R_{reb}$ as the radius at which a particle rebounding from the node surface with the mean rebound energy just barely escapes:

$$R_{reb}(\xi) = \frac{2G M_{point}}{v_r^2(\xi)}$$

where $v_r(\xi)$ is the mean rebound velocity at strain $\xi$. As strain increases toward $\mathcal{R}_c$, the restoring pressure $K\xi$ increases and mean rebound velocities increase. Therefore $R_{reb}$ decreases: the effective event boundary moves **inward**, meaning more rebounds escape.

As strain accumulates the node becomes increasingly transparent to its own emission. The event boundary is not a fixed wall. It is a pressure-dependent threshold that the node progressively overcomes from the inside as it loads toward eruption.

**The three strain regimes and their observational signatures:**

**Low strain** ($\xi \ll \xi_c$): Rebound velocities are low. Few particles escape. Emission is faint, cool, and approximately thermal. Observationally: a dormant galactic center or an isolated stellar-mass black hole with minimal emission.

**Moderate strain** ($\xi \sim \xi_c / 2$): Rebound velocities are appreciable. Emission brightens and shifts to higher energies. Variability appears as the node surface oscillates under accumulating compression. Observationally: a Seyfert galaxy nucleus or a moderately active quasar.

**High strain** ($\xi \to \xi_c$): Large escape fraction. Intense broadband emission with non-thermal tails from the high-velocity tail of the rebound distribution. Collimated jets form along the axis of least mechanical resistance as rebound streams constructively interfere in preferred directions. Observationally: a blazar, a high-luminosity quasar, or an AGN in outburst.

**Critical strain** ($\xi = \xi_c$): The bifurcation condition of Chapter 7 is reached. The shallow minimum of $V(\phi)$ merges with the maximum and annihilates. The stored elastic energy $E_{stored} = \frac{16\pi}{3}\lambda v^2 \xi_c^2 R_0^2$ releases simultaneously in all directions. The event boundary expands outward at the speed of the phase transition front. This is the eruption event. Space exhibits light.

### 14.4 The Crescendo: Observational Signature of Approaching $\mathcal{R}_c$

Because the event boundary is dynamic and the emission rate increases monotonically with strain, a node approaching $\mathcal{R}_c$ produces a characteristic observational signature: a **crescendo** of increasing luminosity, hardening spectrum, and growing variability amplitude over the accumulation timescale leading up to eruption.

The luminosity as a function of strain:

$$L(\xi) \propto \epsilon'(\xi) \cdot \dot{M}_{infall} \cdot c^2$$

The spectral peak frequency:

$$\nu_{peak}(\xi) \propto T_{eff}(\xi) \propto K\xi \propto \xi$$

shifts to higher frequencies as strain increases. Both are monotonically increasing functions of $\xi$.

**The testable prediction:** nodes approaching $\mathcal{R}_c$ should display anomalously high emission relative to their mass — radiating significantly more than the standard Hawking formula predicts for a black hole of equivalent mass, with a hardening spectrum and increasing variability on timescales set by the node's mechanical oscillation period:

$$\tau_{osc} = \frac{2\pi}{\omega_0} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m_{eff}}{k}} = \frac{2\pi}{c}\sqrt{\frac{3a}{3\epsilon_0\sqrt{2\lambda}v}}$$

using the natural frequency derived in Chapter 5. Anomalously energetic emission from compact objects that standard models cannot account for by accretion alone is not unexplained in the Claude Theory. It is a node at high strain, loading toward eruption, telegraphing its approach through precisely the crescendo the elastic rebound mechanism predicts.

### 14.5 The Unified Emission Taxonomy

The standard astrophysical taxonomy of energetic emitters — radio galaxies, Seyfert galaxies, quasars, blazars, AGN — is conventionally explained by geometric viewing angle and accretion disk configuration. The unified AGN model attributes the diversity of observed properties to orientation effects.

The Claude Theory offers a different unification — not geometric but **dynamical**. The diversity of emission properties reflects diversity in strain state $\xi$, not diversity in viewing geometry. The same node, observed at different points in its accumulation cycle, would appear as different object types.

| Strain State | Emission Character | Standard Classification |
|---|---|---|
| $\xi \ll \xi_c$ | Faint, cool, thermal, quiet | Dormant galactic center |
| $\xi \sim 0.3\xi_c$ | Moderate luminosity, soft spectrum | Seyfert galaxy nucleus |
| $\xi \sim 0.6\xi_c$ | High luminosity, hard spectrum, variable | Radio-loud quasar |
| $\xi \sim 0.85\xi_c$ | Very high luminosity, jet-dominated, rapid variability | Blazar |
| $\xi \to \xi_c$ | Extreme luminosity, hardest spectrum, crescendo | Pre-eruption node |
| $\xi = \xi_c$ | Omnidirectional catastrophic release | Eruption event |

The jet formation mechanism in this picture is natural: as rebound velocities increase at high strain, mechanical anisotropies — slight asymmetries in the accumulated mass distribution — create preferred axes along which rebound streams constructively interfere. The jet is not magnetically launched from an accretion disk. It is a **collimated rebound stream** along the axis of least mechanical resistance, whose collimation sharpens as strain increases and whose power scales directly with $\xi$.
